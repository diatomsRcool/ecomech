Pollination

ECOSYSTEM scale
Pollination is the transfer of pollen from the anther of a flower to a compatible stigma, enabling fertilization and seed production in flowering plants. The vast majority of wild plant species and approximately 75% of leading global crop species depend on animal pollinators for reproduction. Pollinators—principally bees (wild native species and managed Apis mellifera), but also butterflies, moths, flies, beetles, birds, and bats—are attracted to flowers by nectar and pollen rewards, inadvertently transferring pollen during foraging. This mutualistic interaction drives plant gene flow, maintains plant population connectivity, and supports ecosystem structure and function through seed and fruit production. Documented declines in wild and managed pollinator populations—driven by habitat loss, agrochemical exposure, pathogens, invasive species, and climate change—threaten both wild plant diversity and global food security.
Also known as: animal pollination, biotic pollination, entomophily, zoophily, bee pollination

Mechanisms

Pollen Transport by Foraging Insects
Bees and other insect pollinators visit flowers to collect nectar and pollen as food rewards. During floral contact, pollen grains adhere to the pollinator body and are carried to subsequent flowers of the same or compatible species, effecting cross-pollination. Pollinator behavior—including floral constancy (visiting one species per foraging bout), visit frequency, and body size relative to flower morphology—determines pollen transfer efficiency. Native bee communities typically include functionally complementary species with different body sizes, tongue lengths, and foraging behaviors that collectively pollinate a broader range of plant species than any single pollinator species.

Pollinator Diversity Provides Functional Insurance for Pollination Services
Native bee communities comprising functionally diverse species provide biological insurance for pollination: when one pollinator species declines, others can compensate through behavioral plasticity and niche overlap. Total pollen deposition on crop flowers is strongly correlated with native bee visitation frequency and community diversity, but not with honey bee visitation alone. The functional complementarity of native bee assemblages— combining species with different body sizes, tongue lengths, and phenologies— ensures robust pollination of plants with diverse floral architectures across spatially and temporally variable landscapes.

Ecological Indicators

Fruit and seed set rate
The proportion of flowers that develop into fruits or seeds (fruit set, seed set) is the most direct indicator of pollination success at the plant level. Pollen-limited species show dramatically reduced fruit/seed set when pollinators are excluded; comparing open-pollinated and hand-pollinated treatments quantifies the degree of pollinator dependence and pollination adequacy in a community.

Pollinator visitation rate and species richness
The number of flower visits per unit time (visitation rate) and the species richness of flower visitors directly quantify pollination service delivery. Standardized transect surveys or time-lapse observation at focal plant patches track pollinator community composition, functional diversity, and visitation frequency — all of which predict effective pollen transfer and plant reproductive success.

Monitoring managed honey bee colony numbers (per region) and wild bee abundance (from transect surveys) tracks the pollinator supply available to agricultural and wild plant communities. Long-term trends in both measures indicate whether pollination services are sustaining, declining, or recovering, particularly as colony losses from disease and pests increase managed bee mortality.

Drivers & Stressors

Habitat loss and landscape fragmentation ANTHROPOGENIC
Conversion of wildflower-rich habitats (hedgerows, meadows, grasslands, woodland margins) to agricultural monocultures removes nesting sites and floral resources for wild bees and other pollinators. Landscape fragmentation isolates pollinator populations and reduces connectivity between remaining habitat patches, limiting recolonization after local extinction. Habitat loss is the dominant driver of wild pollinator declines in agricultural landscapes.

Agrochemicals — insecticides and fungicides ANTHROPOGENIC
Systemic neonicotinoid insecticides applied to crops accumulate in pollen and nectar, exposing bees to sublethal doses that impair navigation, learning, foraging efficiency, and reproduction. Fungicides applied during bloom can reduce pollen nutritional quality and interact synergistically with insecticides to increase pollinator mortality. Agrochemical exposure is a major driver of wild and managed bee colony declines in agricultural landscapes.

Pathogens and parasites (Varroa destructor, Nosema) BIOTIC
Parasitic mites (Varroa destructor) and microsporidian pathogens (Nosema ceranae) are major drivers of managed honey bee colony collapse and stress wild native bee populations. Varroa mites parasitize honey bee pupae and adults, vectoring multiple viruses that individually and synergistically weaken colony immune function. Pathogen-driven colony losses reduce managed pollinator supply and increase reliance on native wild bee communities.

Interventions

Agri-environment schemes to restore wildflower habitat RESTORATION
Policy-supported agri-environment schemes that establish wildflower strips, field margins, hedgerows, and flower-rich meadows in agricultural landscapes restore floral resources and nesting habitat for native bees. Targeted sowing of native wildflower mixes with diverse bloom phenology ensures continuous resource provision across the growing season, supporting functionally diverse pollinator communities capable of providing crop pollination insurance.

Integrated pest management to reduce pollinator exposure to pesticides MITIGATION
Restricting neonicotinoid seed treatments and foliar insecticide applications during crop bloom reduces pollinator exposure to systemic pesticides. Integrated pest management (IPM) approaches that substitute biological controls, crop rotation, and selective chemistry for broad-spectrum insecticides protect pollinator communities while maintaining crop pest control. Regulatory restrictions on neonicotinoid use in the EU have reduced honey bee colony losses in countries with strong enforcement.
