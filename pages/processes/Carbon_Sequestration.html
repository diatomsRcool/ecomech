Carbon Sequestration

ECOSYSTEM scale
Carbon sequestration is the long-term removal and storage of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere into terrestrial, freshwater, and coastal ocean ecosystems. Forests are the dominant terrestrial carbon sink, sequestering 2.4 ± 0.4 petagrams of carbon per year globally through photosynthesis and biomass accumulation. The forest carbon sink comprises both old-growth intact forest (sequestering ~0.85 Pg C yr-1) and regrowing disturbed stands (~1.30 Pg C yr-1). Peatlands, despite covering only ~3% of land area, store approximately one-third of global soil carbon in accumulated Sphagnum litter and other organic material under waterlogged, anaerobic conditions. Vegetated coastal ecosystems (mangroves, salt marshes, seagrasses — collectively "blue carbon") sequester carbon at high rates per unit area in sediment organic matter. Carbon sequestration rates are controlled by primary productivity, decomposition rates, and disturbance regimes; land-use change, deforestation, and peatland drainage are the primary human activities that convert carbon sinks to sources.
Also known as: terrestrial carbon sink, forest carbon sink, blue carbon, peatland carbon accumulation, soil carbon storage

Mechanisms

Forest Biomass Carbon Accumulation
Forests sequester carbon primarily through photosynthetic fixation of CO2 into woody biomass—tree stems, branches, and roots. Net ecosystem production (NEP = gross primary production minus ecosystem respiration) determines the rate of carbon accumulation in living biomass and soil. Intact old-growth forests sequester carbon in increasing biomass stocks, while regrowing forests following harvest or disturbance sequester carbon rapidly during early succession as biomass returns to pre-disturbance levels. Forest demography (age structure, species composition) is a primary determinant of the magnitude and longevity of the forest carbon sink.

Peatland Carbon Accumulation Under Anaerobic Decomposition Limitation
Peatlands sequester carbon through a fundamental imbalance between plant primary production (primarily by Sphagnum mosses and sedges) and decomposition: waterlogged anaerobic conditions suppress microbial decomposition of organic matter, allowing carbon to accumulate as peat over centuries to millennia. Peat deposits typically extend several meters deep and store 10–20 times more carbon per unit area than forests. Drainage of peatlands for agriculture or forestry exposes peat to oxygen, triggering rapid oxidation and releasing centuries of stored carbon. Rewetted peatlands can recover carbon sink status within years, returning to net annual CO2 uptake comparable to intact peatlands.

Coastal Blue Carbon Sequestration in Vegetated Ecosystems
Mangroves, salt marshes, and seagrass beds sequester carbon at rates per unit area among the highest of any ecosystem, primarily through burial of organic matter in anoxic coastal sediments. Sediment carbon stocks in these ecosystems can reach 280 Mg C ha-1 (mangroves) and 250 Mg C ha-1 (tidal marshes) in the top meter — comparable to or exceeding tropical forests despite their small global extent (~49 million ha). Conversion and degradation of these coastal ecosystems releases 0.15–1.02 Pg CO2 per year globally — up to 19% above current deforestation emissions. Protection and restoration of blue carbon ecosystems is therefore a significant but currently underaccounted climate mitigation opportunity.

Ecological Indicators

Net ecosystem carbon balance (NEE, NBP)
Net ecosystem exchange (NEE) measured by eddy covariance towers quantifies the net carbon flux between terrestrial ecosystems and the atmosphere on hourly to decadal timescales. Negative NEE (carbon uptake by ecosystem) indicates active carbon sequestration. Net biome production (NBP) integrates NEE with lateral carbon exports and disturbance emissions to quantify landscape-scale net carbon balance. These flux measurements are the primary direct indicators of ecosystem carbon sequestration rates.

Forest biomass carbon density and stock change
Forest carbon density (Mg C ha-1) and annual stock changes (Mg C ha-1 yr-1) derived from repeated forest inventory measurements quantify forest biomass carbon sequestration. Global forest inventory networks estimate regional and global forest carbon sinks from tree diameter growth and mortality data. Aboveground biomass carbon is the largest and most directly measurable forest carbon pool; soil organic carbon, litter, and coarse woody debris are additional carbon pools measured in comprehensive inventories.

Forest age structure (old-growth vs. regrowth fraction)
The proportion of forest area in old-growth vs. regrowing stands predicts the partitioning of carbon sequestration between stable long-term carbon stocks (old-growth) and large but transient biomass accumulation (regrowth). Satellite-derived and inventory-based forest age maps enable global tracking of the regrowth carbon sink contribution and its expected dynamics as regrowing forests age.

Drivers & Stressors

Deforestation and land-use change ANTHROPOGENIC
Deforestation converts forest carbon sinks to sources, releasing stored biomass and soil carbon to the atmosphere. Gross tropical deforestation emits approximately 2.9 ± 0.5 Pg C yr-1, partially offset by tropical forest regrowth (1.6 Pg C yr-1). The net effect of tropical land-use change is a carbon source of 1.3 ± 0.7 Pg C yr-1. Halting deforestation in primary tropical forests is the single most impactful land-based climate mitigation measure.

Peatland drainage and degradation ANTHROPOGENIC
Drainage of peatlands for agriculture, forestry, and peat extraction exposes accumulated organic carbon to aerobic decomposition, converting peat carbon sinks to large CO2 sources. Degraded tropical peatlands (particularly in Southeast Asia) emit hundreds of millions of tonnes of CO2 per year. Rewetting and restoration of drained peatlands is a cost-effective climate mitigation strategy that rapidly restores carbon sink function.

Climate warming and elevated CO2 CLIMATE
Rising atmospheric CO2 concentrations stimulate photosynthesis and forest growth via CO2 fertilization, contributing to carbon sequestration enhancement in some regions. Conversely, climate warming accelerates ecosystem respiration and disturbance regimes (drought, fire, pest outbreaks), potentially reducing carbon sink capacity. The balance between CO2 fertilization enhancement and warming-driven respiration and disturbance increases will determine the future trajectory of terrestrial carbon sinks under climate change.

Interventions

Forest protection and reforestation MITIGATION
Halting deforestation in intact primary forests protects existing biomass carbon stocks and allows forests to continue sequestering carbon as they grow. Reforestation and afforestation of degraded lands allows new carbon sinks to develop through forest regrowth. Policies including REDD+ (Reduced Emissions from Deforestation and Degradation) provide economic incentives for forest protection in tropical countries.

Peatland rewetting and restoration RESTORATION
Rewetting drained peatlands by blocking drainage ditches and restoring water table levels re-establishes anaerobic conditions that suppress decomposition and restore carbon sequestration. Restored peatlands can return to net CO2 sink status within years of rewetting, with annual uptake rates comparable to intact reference peatlands. Peatland rewetting is recognized as one of the most cost-effective climate change mitigation measures per tonne of CO2 equivalent.

Coastal blue carbon ecosystem protection PROTECTION
Protecting mangroves, salt marshes, and seagrass beds from conversion conserves both ongoing carbon sequestration rates and large standing sediment carbon stocks. Inclusion of blue carbon in national emissions accounting and carbon markets (through REDD+ analogues for coastal ecosystems) creates economic incentives for coastal habitat conservation. Restoration of degraded mangroves and seagrasses additionally expands blue carbon sequestration capacity.
