Redox Dynamics

ECOSYSTEM scale
Redox dynamics in wetland and aquatic sediment ecosystems describes the sequential reduction and oxidation of terminal electron acceptors — oxygen (O2), nitrate (NO3-), iron Fe(III), sulfate (SO42-), and carbon dioxide (CO2) — driven by microbial communities responding to fluctuating water table and oxygen availability. As soils become waterlogged and anaerobic, microbial communities shift through a thermodynamically predictable sequence of electron acceptor utilization, generating the biogeochemical transformations that govern nitrogen loss, phosphorus mobilization, methane production, iron cycling, and plant nutrient availability. Water table fluctuations create spatial and temporal heterogeneity in redox conditions that enables coexistence of diverse microbial functional guilds (aerobic heterotrophs, nitrifiers, denitrifiers, iron reducers, sulfate reducers, methanogens) within close proximity in the soil profile. Radial oxygen loss from wetland plant roots locally converts anaerobic bulk soil to oxic rhizosphere conditions, creating steep micro-scale redox gradients that couple oxidation and reduction of the same elements at millimeter scales, profoundly influencing plant nutrient acquisition, phytotoxin detoxification, and vegetation composition.
Also known as: wetland redox dynamics, soil redox potential dynamics, anaerobic-aerobic cycling, sediment redox chemistry

Mechanisms

Microbial Redox Sequence and N-S-Fe Biogeochemistry
In waterlogged soils, microbial communities sequentially deplete electron acceptors in thermodynamic order: O2, then NO3-, Mn(IV), Fe(III), SO42-, and finally CO2 (methanogenesis). This redox cascade drives cascading biogeochemical transformations: nitrification-denitrification coupling at oxic-anoxic interfaces removes reactive nitrogen as N2 gas; Fe(III) reduction mobilizes phosphorus bound to Fe hydroxides; sulfate reduction generates phytotoxic sulfide; and methanogenesis produces CH4. Rapid changes in water table that shift oxygen availability initiate strong transitions between these microbial functional states, altering nutrient availability and phytotoxin concentrations that directly determine wetland plant growth and vegetation composition.

Anaerobic Fe Redox Cycling in Freshwater Sediments
In freshwater wetland sediments, microbial communities can sustain an anaerobic iron redox cycle by coupling Fe(III) reduction to the oxidation of organic carbon, and subsequently oxidizing the resulting Fe(II) using nitrate as electron acceptor. This anaerobic Fe redox cycle is catalyzed by microorganisms such as Geobacter that are capable of both dissimilatory Fe(III) reduction and nitrate-dependent Fe(II) oxidation, enabling continuous Fe cycling without oxygen. The cycle maintains Fe in biologically available forms, links nitrogen and iron biogeochemistry, and generates local thermodynamic conditions that suppress competing anaerobic processes including methanogenesis.

Redox-Dependent Partitioning of Denitrification Between Fungi and Bacteria
Denitrification — the microbial conversion of nitrate to gaseous N2 and N2O — is carried out by both bacteria and fungi in wetland sediments, but their relative contributions shift predictably along the redox gradient. Under moderately reducing to weakly oxidizing conditions (Eh > +250 mV), fungi dominate denitrification; under strongly reducing conditions (Eh < -100 mV), bacteria dominate. This redox partitioning is ecologically significant because fungal denitrification produces a higher N2O:N2 ratio than bacterial denitrification, meaning that the redox environment determines not only denitrification rate but also the greenhouse gas potency of nitrogen losses from wetland sediments.

Ecological Indicators

Sediment redox potential (Eh)
Redox potential (Eh, millivolts) measured with platinum electrodes in soil or sediment profiles provides the primary physicochemical indicator of wetland redox dynamics. Eh values above +300 mV indicate aerobic conditions; values between +100 and -100 mV indicate transitional redox; values below -200 mV indicate strongly reducing conditions dominated by sulfate reduction and methanogenesis. Time series of Eh at multiple depths tracks the seasonal progression of redox conditions with water table fluctuation.

Measurement: Redox potential (Eh, mV) from platinum electrodes at multiple depths (5, 15, 30 cm) in sediment profiles, paired with water table depth

Dissolved Fe(II) and nitrate concentrations in pore water
Pore water concentrations of dissolved ferrous iron [Fe(II)] and nitrate (NO3-) in wetland sediment profiles serve as biogeochemical indicators of Fe and N redox cycling activity. Elevated Fe(II) indicates active Fe(III) reduction; depletion of NO3- with elevated Fe(II) indicates coupling of Fe reduction to N cycling; temporal dynamics of these concentrations trace the redox sequence as water table rises and falls.

Drivers & Stressors

Water table depth and fluctuation frequency ABIOTIC
Water table depth and its seasonal or event-driven fluctuation frequency is the primary driver of redox dynamics in wetland and riparian ecosystems. Rising water table eliminates O2 from the soil profile and initiates the anaerobic redox sequence; falling water table reintroduces O2 and reverses the sequence. Rapid or frequent fluctuations generate high spatial and temporal redox heterogeneity that supports diverse microbial functional communities and complex biogeochemical cycling.

Organic carbon substrate supply BIOTIC
The availability of labile organic carbon as electron donor determines the rates of anaerobic microbial respiration processes and the extent to which successive electron acceptors are consumed. High organic carbon input (from litter, root exudates, or allochthonous dissolved organic carbon) accelerates oxygen depletion and drives the redox sequence deeper into strongly reducing conditions; low carbon availability maintains soils in intermediate oxidation states with limited methanogenesis.

Interventions

Water table management for redox-mediated nutrient control MANAGEMENT
Managed manipulation of water table depth in constructed or restored wetlands controls sediment redox conditions to optimize desired biogeochemical functions: sustained high water tables promote denitrification and phosphorus immobilization through Fe(III) reduction, while periodic drawdown pulses reoxidize sediments to mineralize organic nitrogen and reset the microbial community. Pulsed water table management is used to balance nitrogen removal, phosphorus retention, and methane emission from treatment wetlands.
