Sulfur Cycling

GLOBAL scale
Sulfur cycling encompasses the biogeochemical transformations of sulfur through its multiple oxidation states (−2 to +6) across terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecosystems. Dissimilatory sulfate reduction by anaerobic microorganisms is the dominant pathway in marine sediments, where sulfate (SO4²⁻) serves as the terminal electron acceptor for organic matter decomposition, producing hydrogen sulfide (H2S). Chemolithotrophic sulfur-oxidizing bacteria and archaea reoxidize reduced sulfur compounds back to sulfate in aerobic and chemocline environments. Volatile organic sulfur compounds (DMS, methanethiol) are important intermediates in ocean-atmosphere sulfur exchange. Anthropogenic emissions of SO2 from fossil fuel combustion have profoundly perturbed the atmospheric sulfur cycle through acid rain deposition.
Also known as: sulfur biogeochemical cycle, S cycle, sulfur biogeochemistry

Mechanisms

Dissimilatory Sulfate Reduction
Sulfate-reducing bacteria and archaea (SRB/SRA) use dissolved sulfate as the terminal electron acceptor for anaerobic oxidation of organic carbon or hydrogen, producing hydrogen sulfide (H2S). This process is the dominant anaerobic decomposition pathway in marine sediments and is estimated to be responsible for up to 50% of organic matter remineralization in coastal sediments. It is one of Earth's oldest metabolic pathways, with isotopic evidence extending back ~3.47 billion years.

Chemolithotrophic Sulfur Oxidation
Sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (SOB) and archaea oxidize reduced sulfur compounds (H2S, elemental sulfur S°, thiosulfate S2O3²⁻, sulfite SO3²⁻) back to sulfate, completing the sulfur redox cycle. Alphaproteobacteria possessing the Sox multienzyme system perform complete oxidation to sulfate; other lineages use alternative pathways. SOB include aerobic chemolithotrophs (Thiobacillus, Beggiatoa), anoxygenic phototrophs (Chlorobium), and endosymbionts.

Volatile Organic Sulfur Compound (VOSC) Cycling
Dimethyl sulfide (DMS) and methanethiol (MT) are the dominant volatile organic sulfur compounds cycling through freshwater and marine ecosystems. In marine systems, DMS originates primarily from enzymatic cleavage of dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP) produced by marine phytoplankton; in freshwater systems, methylation of H2S and degradation of sulfur-containing amino acids are the main production routes. DMS is the largest natural source of atmospheric sulfur and contributes to cloud condensation nuclei formation. Most DMS and MT is degraded anaerobically by methanogens before reaching the atmosphere.

Ecological Indicators

Porewater sulfate concentration profile
Depth profiles of dissolved sulfate in sediment porewater reveal the rate and depth of sulfate reduction; steep gradients indicate active sulfate reduction and are used to calculate sulfate reduction rates via Fick's law.

Hydrogen sulfide flux from sediments
H2S efflux from anoxic sediments to the overlying water column integrates sulfate reduction rates and is a direct indicator of active sulfur cycling. High H2S concentrations create toxic conditions for benthic fauna and indicate hypoxic/euxinic conditions in bottom waters.

Sulfur isotope fractionation (δ34S)
Microbial sulfate reduction preferentially reduces the lighter 32S isotope, leaving sulfate 34S-enriched relative to sulfide. The degree of fractionation (Δδ34S) between coexisting sulfate and sulfide reflects the rate and completeness of sulfate reduction and has been used to reconstruct the history of microbial sulfur cycling in the geological record.

Drivers & Stressors

Organic matter availability ABIOTIC
The supply of labile organic carbon is the primary control on sulfate reduction rates in sediments; organic-rich sediments sustain high rates of dissimilatory sulfate reduction while carbon limitation suppresses sulfur cycling activity.

Oxygen availability and redox stratification ABIOTIC
Dissimilatory sulfate reduction and sulfur oxidation are segregated by redox gradients; sulfate reduction dominates below the oxycline, while sulfur oxidation occurs in the aerobic/microaerobic transition zone. Hypoxia expansion driven by eutrophication and warming extends the zone of active sulfate reduction.

Anthropogenic SO2 emissions and acid deposition ANTHROPOGENIC
Combustion of sulfur-containing fossil fuels emits SO2 to the atmosphere, which is oxidized to sulfate aerosol and deposited as acid rain, acidifying soils and freshwaters and providing excess sulfate substrate to accelerate sulfate reduction in receiving ecosystems.

Sulfate availability in marine vs. freshwater ABIOTIC
Marine sulfate concentration (~28 mM) is ~100-fold higher than typical freshwater concentrations, driving much higher rates of dissimilatory sulfate reduction per unit organic matter in marine sediments compared to freshwater systems, and producing ecosystem-specific VOSC cycling pathways.

Interventions

SO2 emission controls and desulfurization MITIGATION
Flue gas desulfurization (scrubbing) and fuel switching in power generation and industry reduce atmospheric SO2 emissions, decreasing acid sulfate deposition to terrestrial and freshwater ecosystems.

Wetland restoration for sulfur retention RESTORATION
Restoration of drained peatlands and coastal wetlands re-establishes anaerobic conditions that promote sulfate reduction and sulfide mineral precipitation, retaining sulfur in sediment organic complexes rather than allowing oxidized sulfate to leach to aquatic systems.
