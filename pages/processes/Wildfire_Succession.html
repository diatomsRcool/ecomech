ECOCORE:00000194  wildfire succession

Wildfire Succession

ECOSYSTEM scale
Wildfire succession is the directional sequence of community change that follows wildfire disturbance, driven by differential survival, resprouting, and recruitment of plant species with diverse post-fire life-history strategies. Fire removes aboveground biomass, mineralizes nutrients, reduces competition, and creates heterogeneous microsites that together determine post-fire successional trajectories. Overall species richness peaks immediately after fire as disturbance-adapted species colonize burn patches, then declines as canopy closure re-establishes and eliminates early-successional light-demanding species. Successional pathways are determined by fire severity, burn history, post-fire climate, seed source proximity (refugia), and the resprouting versus seeding strategies of resident plant species. Under warming and drying climates, increased fire frequency and post-fire drought stress are altering successional trajectories, converting historically forested landscapes to shrub- or grass-dominated alternative stable states that no longer support tree regeneration.
Also known as: post-fire succession, post-fire vegetation recovery, pyric succession, fire-driven succession, fire succession

Mechanisms

Post-Fire Seed Recruitment and Regeneration Niche
Wildfire creates a distinctive regeneration niche: exposed mineral soil, reduced litter cover, eliminated canopy competition for light, and elevated nutrient availability through ash deposition. Species with fire-adapted germination strategies (heat-stimulated seed release in serotinous cones, smoke-stimulated germination) colonize the burn scar immediately. Tree seedling recruitment is critically sensitive to post-fire weather conditions: the occurrence of even a single extreme drought year immediately after fire substantially suppresses seedling establishment, and severe post-fire drought can cause near-complete recruitment failure that triggers type conversion to non-forest vegetation communities.

Repeated Fire and Altered Forest Recovery
When wildfires recur within short intervals before forest recovery is complete, they fundamentally alter the trajectory of post-fire succession. Repeatedly burned forests accumulate fewer fuels, have sparser tree seedling densities, and can shift toward alternative structural states with reduced canopy closure and basal area. The order of burn severity (low-then-high vs. high-then-low severity) significantly modifies forest recovery, with forests experiencing low severity fire after high severity showing 60% lower canopy closure and 92% fewer tree seedlings. Short fire return intervals may prevent forest recovery entirely, creating persistent non-forest vegetation states.

Climate-Driven Post-Fire Type Conversion
Under warming and drying climate conditions, post-fire forests are increasingly failing to recover their pre-fire forest composition, converting instead to shrub, grassland, or non-forest states. Forest recovery is concentrated at cooler, more mesic sites in proximity to forested refugia that provide seed sources; warmer and drier sites distant from refugia shift toward scrub or grassland community types. Climate models project further decreases in post-fire forest recovery with increasing temperatures, with management opportunities to partially offset conversion through reduced burn severity and maintenance of forested refugia.

Post-Fire Ash Nutrient Pulse and Carbon-Nutrient Interactions in Early Succession
Wildfire mineralizes organic nitrogen and phosphorus in vegetation and soil organic matter into inorganic forms concentrated in the ash layer, creating a transient nutrient pulse that shapes the trajectory of post-fire vegetation recovery. In tundra systems, early post-fire regrowth is limited primarily by canopy photosynthetic potential — set by remaining leaf area — rather than by nutrient availability, because inorganic N and P concentrations in post-fire soils are initially high. Recovery is sustained by uptake of residual inorganic N from ash and by redistribution of N and P from soil organic matter to vegetation. Burn severity mediates this dynamic: moderately burned sites recover faster than severely burned sites despite lower residual ash nutrients, because greater remaining biomass provides higher initial photosynthetic capacity. Within five years, both burn severities can exceed unburned-site aboveground biomass and production, driven by the temporary nutrient enrichment from ash deposition.

Post-Fire Soil Microbial Community Succession
Wildfire dramatically restructures soil microbial communities, reducing bacterial biomass by ~47%, bacterial richness by ~46%, fungal biomass by ~86%, and fungal richness by ~68% immediately after fire. Recovery occurs through rapid secondary succession driven by pyrophilous ('fire-loving') microbes — bacteria and fungi specifically adapted to post-fire conditions through thermotolerance, rapid colonization, and exploitation of fire-derived substrates. Microbial succession is highly dynamic in the first year, with 5–6 distinct compositional turnover periods as early pyrophilous colonizers are replaced by later-successional taxa through competitive interactions. Fungal secondary succession is initiated by the Basidiomycete yeast Geminibasidium, subsequently replaced by filamentous Ascomycetes including Pyronema, Aspergillus, and Penicillium. Because soil microbial communities govern biogeochemical cycling and mediate plant nutrient availability, the trajectory of microbial succession has direct implications for plant community recovery and long-term ecosystem function after wildfire.

Ecological Indicators

Post-fire tree seedling density
Tree seedling density (seedlings per hectare) in burned areas one to five years after wildfire is the primary indicator of forest successional trajectory. High seedling densities indicate successful forest regeneration; low or zero densities indicate recruitment failure and potential type conversion to non-forest vegetation. Seedling density is mapped using plot surveys across burn mosaics stratified by fire severity and post-fire climate conditions.

Drivers & Stressors

Fire severity and extent ABIOTIC
Fire severity (degree of vegetation and soil organic matter consumption) determines the magnitude of post-fire successional reset: high-severity fire that kills most above-ground vegetation and eliminates seed banks creates more extreme successional trajectories than low-severity surface fire that leaves understory vegetation intact. The spatial mosaic of fire severity across a landscape generates diverse successional trajectories that together support high post-fire biodiversity.

Post-fire climate and drought CLIMATE
Post-fire weather conditions, especially drought in the first growing season after fire, are dominant determinants of tree seedling recruitment success and long-term successional trajectory. A single extreme drought year after fire is more damaging to tree recruitment than multiple moderate drought years. Under projected climate warming, post-fire drought frequency and severity are increasing, expanding the landscape fraction vulnerable to post-fire type conversion.

Seed source proximity and fire refugia BIOTIC
Post-fire tree regeneration depends on seed rain from surviving trees at the fire perimeter or within unburned refugia inside the burn area. Large severe burns with few interior refugia have lower seedling densities and slower forest succession because seeds must disperse from distant edges. Under high-severity fire regimes with large contiguous burn patches, seed source limitation becomes a primary constraint on post-fire forest recovery and successional progression.

Interventions

Prescribed fire to restore natural fire regimes RESTORATION
Prescribed burning to restore fire return intervals appropriate to fire-adapted ecosystems (e.g., 2-10 yr intervals in ponderosa pine parklands) maintains low fuel loads, creates the pyrodiversity that sustains post-fire biodiversity, and reduces the probability of high-severity wildfire that triggers type conversion. Prescribed fire reduces the long-term risk of catastrophic post-fire type conversion by maintaining forest structure that supports rapid recovery after the inevitable next wildfire.

Post-fire tree planting in areas of recruitment failure RESTORATION
Where post-fire drought or large burn patch size causes near-complete tree recruitment failure, active reforestation through container seedling planting can supplement natural regeneration and guide succession toward forest rather than shrub or grassland endpoints. Climate-matched seed sourcing and targeting of mesic microsites reduces seedling mortality; reforestation is most effective as a complement to natural regeneration rather than a replacement.
