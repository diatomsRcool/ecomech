Herbivory

ECOSYSTEM scale
Herbivory is the consumption of living plant or algal tissue by animals, coupling primary production to higher trophic levels and regulating plant community structure, biomass, and nutrient cycling. Large mammalian herbivores exert strong top-down control on terrestrial ecosystems, shaping vegetation structure, carbon stocks, hydrological cycles, and fire regimes. Insect herbivores collectively consume comparable quantities of plant biomass and impose intense selective pressure on plant defense chemistry. Plants respond to herbivory with induced chemical and structural defenses mediated by jasmonate signaling cascades and can communicate damage signals to neighboring plants via herbivore-induced plant volatiles. Herbivory drives coevolutionary arms races between plant defense and herbivore counter-adaptation.
Also known as: plant consumption, grazing, browsing, phytophagy

Mechanisms

Large Mammalian Herbivore Top-Down Control of Vegetation
Large mammalian herbivores (body mass ≥100 kg) exert strong top-down control on plant communities by consuming substantial quantities of biomass, transforming landscape structure and reducing above- and below-ground carbon stocks. Through selective foraging, grazing, and browsing they shift plant species composition, alter litter inputs, and modify fire regimes and hydrology, generating cascading effects that extend to carnivores, scavengers, and abiotic ecosystem processes.

Herbivore-Induced Plant Defense
Herbivore attack triggers rapid changes in plant defense chemistry and morphology through jasmonate-mediated signaling cascades. Constitutive defenses—preformed chemical barriers (terpenes, alkaloids, phenolics) and structural features—are supplemented by induced defenses synthesized de novo following damage. Induced plant volatiles additionally recruit natural enemies of herbivores, creating indirect defense pathways that link herbivory to higher trophic levels.

Large Herbivore Nutrient Cycling via Excretion and Landscape Engineering
Large herbivores accelerate nutrient cycling through urine and feces deposition, returning biologically processed plant nutrients to the soil in highly available forms. Concentrated excretion near water sources, wallowing sites, and resting areas creates heterogeneous nutrient hotspots. Trampling modifies soil structure and water infiltration. Collectively, these processes make herbivores major ecosystem engineers affecting vegetation, nutrient cycling, and hydrology.

Herbivore exclusion fences (exclosures) enable direct measurement of net herbivory effects on plant standing biomass, species composition, and landscape greenness (NDVI). Comparison of exclosures versus grazed plots quantifies herbivore top-down control of vegetation across ecosystem types.

Drivers & Stressors

Herbivore population size and diversity ABIOTIC
The density and functional diversity of herbivore communities determines the intensity and selectivity of top-down control on vegetation. High herbivore density increases biomass removal while diverse feeding guilds (grazers, browsers, mixed feeders) create heterogeneous plant community responses.

Plant defense chemistry and palatability ABIOTIC
The constitutive and induced chemical defense levels of plant communities govern herbivore selectivity and consumption rates. High-defense, low- palatability vegetation reduces herbivory; low-defense, high-palatability plants experience intense herbivory, driving community composition shifts.

Hunting and land-use change ANTHROPOGENIC
Anthropogenic hunting, land conversion, and livestock competition are the primary drivers of large herbivore population decline globally. Removal of large herbivores propagates as trophic cascades through the ecosystem, releasing vegetation from top-down control and modifying nutrient cycling, fire regimes, and mesopredator populations.

Interventions

Large carnivore and herbivore rewilding RESTORATION
Reintroducing locally extirpated large herbivores restores top-down vegetation control, nutrient cycling, and ecosystem engineering services. Rewilding projects in Africa, North America, and Europe have demonstrated restoration of plant community structure, nutrient hotspots, and associated food web components following herbivore reintroduction.

Anti-poaching enforcement and protected area management PROTECTION
Strengthening protected area governance, anti-poaching enforcement, and international wildlife trade regulations reduces hunting pressure on large herbivore populations, slowing population decline and range contraction.
