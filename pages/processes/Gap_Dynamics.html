ECOCORE:00000195  gap dynamics

Gap Dynamics

COMMUNITY scale
Gap dynamics describes the formation, light environment, and successional trajectory of canopy gaps created by death or uprooting of individual canopy trees in forest ecosystems. Treefall gaps expose mineral soil and understory vegetation to direct solar radiation, generating high-light regeneration niches that favor recruitment of light-demanding pioneer species unable to establish under closed canopy. The diversity and biomass dynamics of forest stands are substantially determined by the frequency, size, and distribution of gaps across the landscape. Gap size controls the duration and intensity of the elevated light environment: large gaps support rapid growth of sun-demanding species and lianas, while small gaps are quickly recolonized by shade-tolerant species. Liana competition in gaps is a key biotic filter that suppresses shade-tolerant tree recruitment and reduces tree richness, revealing that gap-phase regeneration is shaped by both the abiotic light environment and competitive interactions among plant growth forms.
Also known as: treefall gap dynamics, gap-phase regeneration, canopy gap dynamics, gap disturbance

Mechanisms

Gap Light Environment and Pioneer Recruitment
Treefall creates patches of elevated solar irradiance at the forest floor — the gap light environment — that act as a regeneration niche for light-demanding pioneer and early-successional tree species unable to recruit under closed canopy shade. Seedfall composition into gaps differs qualitatively from that in closed-canopy areas: wind-dispersed and autochorous species are enriched in gap seedfall, while zoochorous species dominate among established saplings. The resulting sapling cohorts in gaps are compositionally distinct and less diverse than cohorts under intact canopy, but contain many gap-specialist species that contribute to overall stand-level tree diversity. Gap size, definition criteria, and spatial scale of measurement strongly affect inferred gap disturbance parameters and their inferred roles in above-ground biomass and species coexistence.

Liana Competition Suppresses Tree Recruitment in Gaps
Lianas exploit the high-light environment of treefall gaps more rapidly than tree seedlings and suppress tree growth, recruitment, and richness through biotic interference. Experimental liana removal from gaps demonstrates that liana competition, rather than dispersal limitation alone, constrains shade-tolerant tree recruitment in gaps. Lianas are disproportionately harmful to shade-tolerant species while leaving light-demanding pioneers relatively unaffected, revealing a mechanistic link between liana abundance and the failure of gaps to maintain shade-tolerant tree diversity as predicted by classical gap-phase regeneration theory.

Ecological Indicators

Canopy gap fraction
The proportion of forest area occupied by canopy gaps, measured from hemispherical photography, LiDAR canopy height models, or satellite imagery. Gap fraction integrates gap disturbance frequency and size distribution, and is the primary landscape-scale indicator of gap dynamics intensity. High gap fraction indicates active gap-phase regeneration and associated pioneer recruitment.

Light-demanding sapling density in gaps
Density of light-demanding pioneer tree saplings (seedlings to 1 cm dbh) within gap areas compared to closed-canopy areas. High density of pioneer species in gaps and their absence under closed canopy confirms active gap-phase regeneration. Species composition of gap sapling cohorts, quantified by Fisher's alpha or species richness, indicates whether gaps are supporting their expected diversity function.

Drivers & Stressors

Canopy tree mortality rate ABIOTIC
The rate at which canopy trees die through windthrow, senescence, pathogen attack, or drought stress determines the frequency and spatial density of gap formation. Higher mortality rates generate more frequent gaps and sustain a higher steady-state gap fraction across the forest landscape. Disturbance regimes (hurricanes, ice storms, bark beetle outbreaks) create episodic pulses of gap formation that temporarily increase gap fraction above background levels.

Liana abundance BIOTIC
Liana cover and stem density in forest understories determines the magnitude of biotic suppression of tree recruitment in treefall gaps. High liana abundance amplifies the liana interference mechanism, converting gaps from tree diversity hotspots to liana-dominated patches with suppressed tree regeneration. Liana abundance is increasing in many tropical forests, potentially altering the classic gap-phase regeneration dynamic.

Interventions

Liana cutting in restoration gaps RESTORATION
Manual cutting of lianas in treefall gaps or deliberately created gaps releases tree seedlings and saplings from competitive suppression and accelerates gap-phase tree regeneration toward desired species composition. Liana cutting is most effective when combined with targeted planting of desired tree species in gaps; repeat cutting may be required because lianas resprout rapidly after cutting.
