Marine Benthic Primary Production

ECOSYSTEM scale
Marine benthic primary production is the fixation of CO2 into organic biomass by photosynthetic organisms living on or in seafloor sediments, including microphytobenthos (MPB), seagrasses, macroalgae, and benthic cyanobacteria. Globally, benthic microalgal productivity reaches ~500 million tons of carbon per year, with estuarine MPB contributing 27-234 g C m-2 yr-1. Benthic primary production is critical to coastal and estuarine food webs and sediment carbon dynamics. Key controls include light penetration through the water column, sediment disturbance, tidal exposure, and nutrient availability. Unlike pelagic production, benthic primary producers experience steep physical and chemical gradients and are subjected to highly variable tidal regimes.
Also known as: benthic primary production, microphytobenthos production, MPB primary production, seafloor primary production

Mechanisms

Microphytobenthos (MPB) Photosynthesis
Sediment-attached assemblages of diatoms, cyanobacteria, and other microalgae—collectively termed microphytobenthos—are the dominant primary producers in intertidal and shallow subtidal environments. MPB diatoms perform photosynthesis within the upper millimeters of illuminated sediment, often migrating vertically in response to tidal cycles and light gradients to optimize light and carbon availability. Global annual benthic microalgal productivity reaches 500 million tons of carbon.

Carbon Limitation of Benthic Photosynthesis
Unlike pelagic phytoplankton, benthic diatoms in dense MPB mats experience rapid drawdown of dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) within the photic sediment layer, creating localized carbon limitation that constrains photosynthetic rates. Vertical migration of motile diatoms may partially overcome this limitation by shuttling cells between carbon-depleted surface layers and carbon-replete deeper sediment.

MPB Contribution to Estuarine Carbon and Food Web Dynamics
Microphytobenthos production supports benthic invertebrate food webs and contributes substantially to estuarine autochthonous carbon fixation. In many estuaries, MPB primary production rivals or exceeds phytoplankton production per unit area, making benthic production a critical subsidy to pelagic food webs via benthic-pelagic coupling.

Sediment surface chlorophyll-a
Chlorophyll-a concentration in the upper few millimeters of sediment is the standard proxy for MPB biomass; measured by in situ fluorometry, spectroradiometry, or laboratory extraction from sediment cores.

Drivers & Stressors

Light attenuation through the water column ABIOTIC
Turbidity, water depth, and particulate matter in the overlying water column attenuate downwelling irradiance, setting the lower depth limit for benthic photosynthesis. High turbidity is the primary reason MPB production is concentrated in shallow intertidal and subtidal zones.

Tidal exposure and desiccation ABIOTIC
Tidal cycles alternately expose intertidal sediments to aerial conditions (promoting photosynthesis and desiccation stress) and submerge them (enabling nutrient and DIC resupply). The timing and duration of emersion regulates MPB productivity and triggers vertical migration behavior.

Sediment disturbance and resuspension ABIOTIC
Physical disturbance by waves, currents, bioturbation, and trawling resuspends surface sediments, disrupting MPB biofilms and transferring benthic primary producers to the water column where they are grazed or exported. Reduced disturbance promotes MPB biomass accumulation.

Interventions

Reduction of coastal turbidity from land runoff RESTORATION
Reducing sediment and nutrient runoff from agricultural and urban land decreases water column turbidity in estuaries and coastal zones, increasing light penetration to benthic habitats and expanding the depth range and productivity of MPB and seagrass communities.

Reduction of bottom trawling in shallow coastal zones PROTECTION
Banning or restricting bottom trawling in shallow coastal and estuarine zones reduces chronic sediment disturbance and MPB biofilm disruption, allowing benthic primary producer communities to recover and accumulate biomass.
