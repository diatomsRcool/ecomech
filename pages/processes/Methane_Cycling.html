Methane Cycling

ECOSYSTEM scale
Methane cycling in wetland and aquatic ecosystems encompasses the production of methane (CH4) by methanogenic archaea under strictly anaerobic conditions (methanogenesis), its consumption by methanotrophic bacteria at oxic-anoxic interfaces (methanotrophy), and its emission to the atmosphere through diffusion, ebullition, and vascular plant transport. Wetlands are the single largest natural source of atmospheric CH4, contributing approximately 164 Tg CH4 yr-1 — about one third of total global emissions. Net wetland CH4 flux is primarily controlled by soil temperature, water table depth, and vegetation type, with methanogenesis increasing steeply with temperature due to its unusually high activation energy (0.96 eV) relative to photosynthesis (0.3 eV) or aerobic respiration (0.65 eV). This temperature sensitivity means that global warming will disproportionately increase CH4 emissions relative to CO2 from aquatic ecosystems, altering the ratio of CH4 to CO2 greenhouse gas emissions. Water table position governs the balance between methanogenesis in the anaerobic zone and methanotrophy in the aerobic zone, while vascular plant aerenchyma both delivers oxygen to suppress methanogenesis and provides a high-conductance pathway for CH4 transport directly to the atmosphere, bypassing methanotrophic oxidation.
Also known as: wetland methane cycling, methanogenesis, CH4 biogeochemistry, methane flux dynamics, anaerobic carbon mineralization

Mechanisms

Methanogenesis as Terminal Step in Anaerobic Carbon Mineralization
Methanogenesis is carried out by strictly anaerobic methanogenic archaea that consume hydrogen (H2/CO2 pathway) or acetate as substrates in the terminal step of organic matter mineralization under anaerobic conditions. Like other forms of microbial metabolism, methanogenesis is temperature- dependent, but its activation energy (0.96 eV) is substantially higher than aerobic respiration (~0.65 eV) and photosynthesis (~0.3 eV), predicting a 57-fold increase in methanogenesis between 0°C and 30°C. This steep temperature dependence propagates from pure methanogen cultures through anaerobic microbial communities to the ecosystem scale, where seasonal CH4 emissions show the same average temperature sensitivity — demonstrating that physiological temperature response of methanogens constrains ecosystem-scale CH4 flux across all wetland types.

Water Table and Temperature as Dominant Controls on Wetland CH4 Flux
Net CH4 flux from wetlands is primarily governed by the interaction between water table position, which determines the depth and extent of the anaerobic methanogenic zone, and temperature, which controls the rate of methanogenesis. The optimal water table depth for maximum CH4 flux differs among wetland types: in bogs, maximum flux occurs when dry antecedent conditions are followed by wet conditions; in fens and swamps, maximum flux occurs under sustained wet conditions both 30-day antecedent and current. Fen methane flux is more sensitive to vegetation and less sensitive to temperature than bog or swamp flux, demonstrating that vascular plant community composition modifies the dominant physical controls on CH4 emissions. Drained wetlands show distinctly different CH4–environment relationships from pristine wetlands, confirming that the water table–CH4 relationship is not transferable across hydrological states.

Wetland Heterogeneity in Global CH4 Emissions
Wetlands are the largest single natural source of atmospheric CH4, but emission rates are highly heterogeneous among and within wetland types, regions, and disturbance states. Fen CH4 flux differs mechanistically from bog or swamp flux in its greater sensitivity to vegetation (vascular plant transport) and lower sensitivity to temperature. Drained wetlands exhibit characteristic loss of the large CH4 flux events that occur under warm-wet conditions in pristine wetlands, demonstrating that disturbance fundamentally alters the CH4–environment relationship. Global and regional CH4 emission estimates carry large uncertainties from spatial heterogeneity of wetlands, inadequate wetland mapping, and the limited number of observational sites relative to the diversity of wetland types and climate regions.

Ecological Indicators

Net CH4 flux from wetland surfaces
Net CH4 flux to the atmosphere (mg CH4 m-2 d-1) measured by static chambers, eddy covariance towers, or automated chamber systems is the primary indicator of methane cycling intensity. Flux measurements integrate methanogenesis, methanotrophy, and transport by diffusion, ebullition, and vascular plants. Flux partitioned by season and stratified by water table depth and temperature identifies the dominant environmental controls on methane cycling at a given site.

Pore water CH4 concentration profile
Dissolved CH4 concentration in soil pore water at multiple depths indicates the depth and intensity of the methanogenic zone and diffusive CH4 flux gradients. High pore water CH4 at depth with a steep gradient toward the surface indicates active methanogenesis below the water table and diffusive transport to the atmosphere; the profile shape also indicates whether methanotrophic consumption at the oxic-anoxic interface is significant.

Drivers & Stressors

Soil temperature CLIMATE
Soil temperature in the anaerobic zone is a primary driver of methanogenesis rate, with an unusually high activation energy (0.96 eV) that translates to a 57-fold increase in methanogenesis between 0°C and 30°C. This high temperature sensitivity is consistent across microbial cultures, anaerobic communities, and ecosystem-scale seasonal CH4 emissions. Under global warming, temperature increases in northern peatlands will drive disproportionate increases in CH4 emissions relative to CO2, altering the greenhouse gas balance of boreal and arctic wetlands.

Water table depth ABIOTIC
Water table position determines the depth and spatial extent of the anaerobic methanogenic zone; high water tables that saturate most of the soil profile maximize methanogenic zone volume and minimize methanotrophic oxidation by reducing the depth of the aerobic zone. In bogs, the largest CH4 flux events occur when dry antecedent conditions (compressed peat, limited methanogenesis) are followed by rewetting; in fens and swamps, sustained wet conditions maximize flux. Drought-induced water table drawdown reduces CH4 emissions by expanding the aerobic methanotrophic zone.

Vegetation type and vascular plant community BIOTIC
Vascular plant communities with aerenchyma tissue (sedges, reeds, rice, wetland emergents) simultaneously supply oxygen to root zones (locally suppressing methanogenesis) and provide high-conductance pathways for CH4 transport directly from rhizospheric production sites to the atmosphere, bypassing methanotrophic oxidation. Fen CH4 flux shows greater sensitivity to vegetation than bog or swamp flux, demonstrating that vascular plant transport can override temperature and water table controls in some wetland types.

Interventions

Wetland rewetting to restore CH4 cycling and carbon balance RESTORATION
Rewetting of drained peatlands by blocking drainage ditches and raising water tables restores anaerobic conditions that support methanogenesis and enable carbon accumulation. While rewetting initially increases CH4 emissions, the restored carbon sequestration in accumulating peat typically offsets the increased CH4 greenhouse gas forcing on decadal timescales. Rewetting is the primary intervention for restoring carbon and greenhouse gas balance of degraded peatland ecosystems.

Managed water table drawdown to reduce CH4 emissions MANAGEMENT
Strategic water table drawdown in managed wetlands (rice paddies, treatment wetlands, constructed peatlands) during periods of high CH4 production can reduce net emissions by expanding the aerobic methanotrophic zone and temporarily oxidizing CH4 produced during high-water phases. Mid-season drainage in rice paddies is a widely implemented agronomic practice that reduces CH4 emissions while maintaining grain yields.
