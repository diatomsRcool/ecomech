Phosphorus Cycling

ECOSYSTEM scale
Phosphorus cycling encompasses the biogeochemical transformations of phosphorus through terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecosystems. Unlike nitrogen, there is no atmospheric reservoir; P enters ecosystems through weathering of primary minerals (mainly apatite), cycles through biological uptake, mineralization of organic forms, and is lost via leaching and erosion. Mycorrhizal fungi, phosphate- solubilizing microorganisms, and extracellular phosphatases are the primary biological agents of P mobilization. Human activities have massively altered the global P cycle through mining of phosphate rock and application of P fertilizers in agriculture.
Also known as: phosphorus biogeochemical cycle, P cycle, phosphorus biogeochemistry

Mechanisms

Geochemical Weathering and Mineral Phosphorus Release
Physical and chemical dissolution of phosphate-bearing primary minerals (principally apatite, Ca5(PO4)3(OH/F/Cl)) releases inorganic orthophosphate (Pi) into soil solution. Weathering rate controls the long-term ecosystem P budget and is the primary mechanism of P input on millennial timescales.

Biological Mineralization of Organic Phosphorus
Decomposer microorganisms and plant roots release extracellular phosphatase enzymes (phosphomonoesterases, phosphodiesterases, phytases) that hydrolyze organic phosphate esters to inorganic orthophosphate, constituting the primary internal recycling pathway for organically bound P in soils.

Mycorrhizal Phosphorus Uptake
Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and ectomycorrhizal fungi form symbioses with plant roots, extending the effective P depletion zone through hyphal networks that access soil pores too small for roots. Fungi transfer P to the host plant in exchange for photosynthate, often dominating the total P supply to the plant irrespective of growth response magnitude.

Soil resin-extractable phosphorus
Resin-extractable inorganic P in surface soils provides an integrative measure of plant-available P, capturing the labile Pi pool in equilibrium with soil solution. A key indicator of P limitation status and fertilization history.

Drivers & Stressors

Soil parent material age and P content ABIOTIC
Long-term pedogenesis progressively depletes primary mineral P, driving a shift from N limitation in young soils to P limitation in old, highly weathered soils. Parent material P content sets the ceiling for ecosystem P inputs over geological timescales.

Agricultural phosphorus fertilization ANTHROPOGENIC
Application of inorganic P fertilizers (superphosphate, triple superphosphate, MAP/DAP) and animal manure massively increases P inputs to agricultural soils, creating large P surpluses in intensive systems while P deficits persist in subsistence farming regions.

Soil pH and iron/aluminum oxide chemistry ABIOTIC
Soil pH controls the speciation and sorption of phosphate; P is most available near pH 6–7 and strongly sorbed (fixed) at acidic pH by Fe/Al oxides and at alkaline pH by Ca precipitation, creating soil barriers to P bioavailability independent of total P content.

Runoff of agricultural P to freshwater lakes and coastal zones elevates dissolved P concentrations, driving algal blooms, anoxia, and the loss of aquatic biodiversity.

Interventions

Manure phosphorus recycling MANAGEMENT
Returning animal manure to croplands as an organic P fertilizer substitutes for mined phosphate rock, closes regional P cycles, and reduces P surplus in intensive livestock regions while alleviating P deficits in crop-only systems.
