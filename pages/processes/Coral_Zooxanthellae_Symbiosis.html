Coral-Zooxanthellae Symbiosis

ECOSYSTEM scale
Coral-zooxanthellae symbiosis is the obligate mutualistic association between reef-building scleractinian corals (Cnidaria: Scleractinia) and intracellular dinoflagellate microalgae of the family Symbiodiniaceae (informally called zooxanthellae), which is fundamental to the productivity and structural complexity of tropical coral reef ecosystems. Zooxanthellae reside within membrane-bound vacuoles (symbiosomes) in coral gastrodermal cells, where they photosynthesize and translocate the majority of their photosynthate to the coral host as sugars, glycerol, and amino acids. This photosynthetic subsidy powers coral calcification, growth, and reproduction in otherwise nutrient-poor (oligotrophic) tropical oceans. In return, the coral provides the algae with metabolic CO2, nitrogen, phosphorus, and a protected intracellular niche. Thermal stress disrupts this intimate association, causing bleaching—the expulsion of zooxanthellae from coral tissues—and threatening reef survival globally as ocean temperatures rise.
Also known as: coral symbiosis, zooxanthellate coral, Symbiodiniaceae symbiosis, photosymbiosis in corals, cnidarian-dinoflagellate symbiosis

Mechanisms

Photosynthate Transfer from Zooxanthellae to Coral Host
Intracellular Symbiodiniaceae photosynthesize using solar energy penetrating the coral tissue and translocate the majority of their photosynthetically fixed carbon (sugars, glycerol, amino acids) to the coral host. This photosynthate subsidy can meet 90-100% of coral energy requirements under optimal light conditions, enabling calcification and rapid growth in the nutrient-poor tropical ocean. Zooxanthellae are functionally phototrophic within the animal host, and the coral host adopts convergent physiological adaptations to maximize light capture (morphology changes, carbon concentrating mechanisms, UV screening).

Thermal Bleaching — Symbiosis Disruption by Ocean Warming
When sea surface temperatures exceed the thermal tolerance of the symbiosis by 1-2°C above the seasonal maximum for sustained periods (degree heating weeks, DHW), oxidative stress damages the photosynthetic machinery of zooxanthellae and triggers their expulsion from coral tissues—coral bleaching. Bleached corals lose their photosynthetic energy supply and rely entirely on heterotrophy for energy, which can sustain corals for weeks but leads to mortality under prolonged or repeated bleaching. Global warming is increasing bleaching frequency and severity, threatening coral reef persistence.

Coral Heterotrophy as Backup Energy During Bleaching
Corals are mixotrophic: in addition to zooxanthellae-derived photosynthate, many coral species actively capture zooplankton and particulate organic matter. During bleaching, when photosynthate supply drops to near zero, heterotrophic feeding can supply up to 100% of metabolic requirements and provides nitrogen and phosphorus not available from zooxanthellae. Increased heterotrophy during bleaching can delay mortality and accelerate recovery if temperatures return to normal, but cannot substitute fully for zooxanthellae carbon supply over extended bleaching events.

Bleaching extent (% of reef area affected) and severity (% of colony surface bleached, measured on a 5-point visual scale) quantify symbiosis disruption during thermal stress events. Aerial and underwater surveys combined with satellite-derived sea surface temperature anomalies provide synoptic assessment of bleaching events.

Degree heating weeks (DHW)
Degree heating weeks integrate the magnitude and duration of sea surface temperature anomalies above the local maximum monthly mean. DHW thresholds of ≥4 DHW predict bleaching and ≥8 DHW predict significant mortality; DHW is the primary satellite-derived indicator of bleaching risk and coral reef thermal stress globally.

Drivers & Stressors

Ocean warming and marine heat waves CLIMATE
Sea surface temperature anomalies driven by global climate change and ENSO events are the dominant driver of coral bleaching and symbiosis disruption. Even small sustained temperature increases (1-2°C above seasonal maximum) are sufficient to trigger mass bleaching; the frequency and severity of thermal events is increasing with global warming.

Ocean acidification ANTHROPOGENIC
Anthropogenic CO2 absorption by the ocean reduces seawater pH and carbonate ion concentration, impeding coral calcification and weakening reef structural integrity. While distinct from bleaching, acidification interacts synergistically with warming to reduce coral resilience and recovery from bleaching events.

Local stressors (sedimentation, eutrophication, fishing) ANTHROPOGENIC
Local stressors including sedimentation, coastal eutrophication, and overfishing can reduce coral health and bleaching resistance through elevated turbidity (reducing light availability for zooxanthellae), nutrient enrichment favoring algal overgrowth, and disruption of herbivore fish communities. However, local protection does not prevent thermally-induced bleaching from ocean warming.

Interventions

Global greenhouse gas emission reduction MITIGATION
Limiting global average temperature increase to 1.5°C through rapid decarbonization is the only intervention capable of maintaining coral reef systems at ecological scale. At 2°C warming, projections indicate 70-90% loss of coral reefs; below 1.5°C, approximately 10-30% of reefs are projected to survive thermally-induced bleaching events.

Coral reef protected area management and local stressor reduction PROTECTION
Marine protected areas (MPAs) reduce local stressors, maintain herbivore fish communities that suppress algal overgrowth post-bleaching, and improve reef recovery rates between bleaching events. Local protection cannot prevent bleaching from ocean warming but can extend the window of reef survival under moderate warming scenarios.
