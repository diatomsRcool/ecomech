Iron Cycling

ECOSYSTEM scale
Iron cycling is the biogeochemical transformation of iron between its ferric (Fe(III)) and ferrous (Fe(II)) oxidation states, mediated by microorganisms in soils, sediments, and aquatic environments. Iron is the fourth most abundant element in the Earth's crust, and its redox cycling drives carbon and nutrient transformations in anaerobic environments where Fe(III) serves as a terminal electron acceptor for microbial respiration. Dissimilatory iron-reducing bacteria such as Geobacter and Shewanella couple the oxidation of organic matter to the reduction of Fe(III) oxides, releasing ferrous iron and driving phosphorus mobilization, trace metal transformations, and methane suppression. Iron-oxidizing bacteria re-oxidize Fe(II) under both oxic and anoxic (nitrate-dependent) conditions, completing the iron redox cycle. Biogeochemical iron cycling is coupled to the carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur cycles and exerts strong controls on greenhouse gas emissions and contaminant fate in soils and sediments.
Also known as: iron biogeochemical cycle, Fe redox cycling, dissimilatory iron reduction, microbial iron cycling

Mechanisms

Dissimilatory Fe(III) Reduction by Anaerobic Microorganisms
Under anaerobic conditions in waterlogged soils and sediments, dissimilatory iron-reducing bacteria use Fe(III) minerals (iron oxides, hydroxides) as terminal electron acceptors for respiration, oxidizing organic compounds (acetate, hydrogen, aromatic compounds) while producing Fe(II). This process is the dominant pathway of organic matter mineralization in Fe(III)-rich anaerobic environments and was potentially the first globally significant mechanism of organic matter oxidation on Earth. Key organisms include Geobacter sulfurreducens and other Deltaproteobacteria. Fe(III) reduction controls phosphorus release from sediments, drives trace metal mobilization, and generates magnetite minerals with diagnostic geochemical signatures.

Anaerobic Iron Redox Cycling — Coupled Fe(III) Reduction and Fe(II) Oxidation
In freshwater wetland sediments, iron undergoes complete anaerobic redox cycling: Fe(III) oxides are reduced to Fe(II) by iron-reducing bacteria (e.g., Geobacter) consuming acetate, and the resulting Fe(II) is then re-oxidized to Fe(III) by nitrate-reducing iron-oxidizing bacteria (e.g., Dechloromonas) when nitrate becomes available. This coupled cycling regenerates Fe(III) minerals in situ, sustaining iron reduction activity and creating a dynamic iron redox shuttle that links carbon, nitrogen, and iron cycles within anoxic microsites.

Iron Cycling Linkages to Carbon, Nutrient, and Contaminant Transformations
Iron redox reactions are coupled to multiple biogeochemical cycles: Fe(III) reduction promotes organic carbon mineralization in anaerobic soils, Fe(II) oxidation supports chemolithotrophic carbon fixation, and iron cycling controls phosphorus mobility through sorption and co-precipitation with Fe(III) oxides. Iron oxides also sorb and immobilize toxic metals and metalloids (arsenic, chromium), and their reductive dissolution releases these contaminants into groundwater. Novel processes recently documented include methane oxidation and ammonium oxidation coupled to Fe(III) reduction, expanding the scope of iron-driven biogeochemical transformations.

Ecological Indicators

Soil or sediment Fe(II)/Fe(III) ratio
The ratio of ferrous (Fe(II)) to ferric (Fe(III)) iron in soil or sediment porewater directly reflects iron cycling intensity and redox conditions. High Fe(II)/Fe(III) ratios indicate active dissimilatory iron reduction under anaerobic conditions; seasonal and spatial variation in this ratio traces iron redox cycling dynamics in wetland soils and freshwater sediments.

Drivers & Stressors

Soil or sediment anaerobiosis and redox potential ABIOTIC
The onset of anaerobic conditions (waterlogging, flooding, oxygen depletion) is the primary environmental trigger for dissimilatory iron reduction. Redox potential (Eh) drops to approximately +100 to -100 mV when iron reduction becomes the dominant terminal electron-accepting process. Seasonally fluctuating water tables drive alternating Fe(III) reduction and Fe(II) oxidation cycles in wetland soils.

Organic carbon availability ABIOTIC
Labile organic carbon (acetate, hydrogen, fatty acids) is the primary electron donor for dissimilatory iron reduction; without adequate carbon substrate, iron-reducing bacteria cannot sustain Fe(III) reduction. Organic matter quality and quantity in sediments controls iron cycling rates, coupling iron cycling tightly to organic matter decomposition and primary productivity inputs.

Fe(III) oxide mineral availability and crystallinity ABIOTIC
The quantity and crystallinity of Fe(III) oxide minerals in soils and sediments determines the electron-accepting capacity available for dissimilatory iron reduction. Poorly crystalline (amorphous) ferrihydrite is more bioavailable than crystalline goethite and hematite. Weathering of parent rock, atmospheric deposition, and biological mineralization determine Fe(III) oxide stocks in different ecosystems.

Interventions

Wetland restoration to restore iron-driven biogeochemistry RESTORATION
Rewetting of drained wetlands and peatlands restores anaerobic conditions that enable dissimilatory iron reduction, reactivating iron cycling and its downstream effects on carbon preservation, phosphorus retention, and methane suppression. Iron cycling restoration is a key mechanism by which rewetted wetlands recover their carbon sequestration and nutrient retention functions.

Bioremediation using iron-reducing bacteria for contaminant immobilization RESTORATION
Dissimilatory iron-reducing bacteria (particularly Geobacter species) can be stimulated in situ by organic carbon amendment to accelerate Fe(III) reduction, promoting reductive precipitation and immobilization of uranium, chromium, arsenic, and other redox-sensitive contaminants. Biostimulation of iron cycling has been developed as a site remediation strategy for metal-contaminated groundwater and sediments.
