Mycorrhizal Association

ECOSYSTEM scale
Mycorrhizal symbiosis is the ancient, ubiquitous mutualistic association between soil fungi and plant roots in which the fungus provides the plant with mineral nutrients—primarily phosphorus and nitrogen—in exchange for photosynthetically fixed carbon. Mycorrhizal fungi colonize over 90% of all plant species and mediate a large fraction of global terrestrial nutrient cycling and primary production. Two major forms dominate: arbuscular mycorrhizas (AM), formed by Glomeromycota fungi and present in most crop plants and herbaceous vegetation since the Ordovician, and ectomycorrhizas (EM), formed by Basidiomycota and Ascomycota fungi and dominant in boreal and temperate forest trees. Mycorrhizal networks connect individual plants belowground, enabling carbon and nutrient transfer among trees and potentially linking seedling establishment to mature forest canopy.
Also known as: mycorrhiza, arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis, ectomycorrhizal symbiosis, mycorrhizal mutualism, plant-fungal symbiosis

Mechanisms

Bidirectional Nutrient-Carbon Exchange at the Plant-Fungal Interface
At the plant-fungal interface, mycorrhizal fungi deliver phosphorus, nitrogen, and other mineral nutrients to plant root cells while receiving photosynthetically fixed carbon (sugars) from the plant. This exchange occurs via specialized structures—arbuscules in AM symbiosis, Hartig net in EM symbiosis—lined with novel nutrient transporter proteins identified in both partners. The carbon cost to the plant can range from 4-20% of net photosynthate; in return, fungal hyphae extend the plant's nutrient acquisition volume far beyond the depletion zone of root hair uptake.

Mycorrhizal Network Carbon Transfer Among Plants
Common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs) form when multiple plants are colonized by the same fungal mycelium, creating belowground connections that can mediate carbon transfer between host trees. Carbon transfer from large donor trees to shaded seedlings via CMNs has been demonstrated in both laboratory and field settings. The ecological significance of CMN-mediated carbon transfer for seedling establishment and forest regeneration under low light is an active area of research, with accumulating field evidence supporting the phenomenon.

Ancient Co-evolutionary Origin and Universal Land Plant Colonization
Arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis is one of the oldest plant-microbe interactions, with fossil evidence from the Ordovician (~460 Ma) indicating that mycorrhizal fungi may have facilitated the colonization of land by early plants. The conservation of plant cell responses to AM colonization across haploid gametophytes, diploid sporophytes, and diverse seed plant clades implies a shared ancestral molecular program recruited early in land plant evolution. This ancient co-evolutionary origin explains the near-universal distribution of mycorrhizal symbiosis among land plants.

Ecological Indicators

Mycorrhizal colonization rate
The percentage of root length or root tips colonized by mycorrhizal fungi (AM or EM) quantifies the intensity of plant-fungal symbiosis. Higher colonization rates reflect greater fungal investment in the symbiosis and predict higher nutrient transfer rates to the plant.

Drivers & Stressors

Soil phosphorus availability ABIOTIC
Mycorrhizal colonization and symbiotic benefit to plants are strongest in phosphorus-poor soils. As soil P availability increases (e.g., via fertilization), plants reduce carbon allocation to mycorrhizal fungi, weakening the mutualism. P fertilization is the primary driver suppressing mycorrhizal symbiosis in agricultural systems.

Soil disturbance and agricultural intensification ANTHROPOGENIC
Tillage disrupts the extraradical hyphal networks and spore banks of AM fungi, reducing colonization potential. Phosphorus fertilization further suppresses mycorrhizal symbiosis. Fungicide and broad-spectrum pesticide use reduce mycorrhizal fungal diversity. These factors combine in intensive agriculture to strongly reduce mycorrhizal colonization and associated ecosystem services.

Interventions

Mycorrhizal inoculant application in restoration and agriculture RESTORATION
Applying mycorrhizal fungal inoculants to seedlings during restoration plantings or to seeds before sowing can establish symbiosis in mycorrhizal- depleted soils (post-agricultural, severely disturbed sites). Effective inoculation improves seedling survival, growth, and drought tolerance, reducing fertilizer requirements in restoration and sustainable agriculture.

Reduced P fertilization to restore mycorrhizal function MANAGEMENT
Reducing or eliminating phosphorus fertilization in agricultural and managed grassland systems allows mycorrhizal colonization to recover, restoring fungal nutrient uptake capacity and reducing dependency on chemical P inputs. Integrated nutrient management combining lower P fertilizer with mycorrhizal management can maintain crop yields while improving soil fungal biodiversity.
