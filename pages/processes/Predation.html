Predation

ECOSYSTEM scale
Predation is the consumption of living prey by a predator, coupling energy flow between trophic levels and regulating prey population dynamics, community structure, and ecosystem processes through top-down control. Apex predators—large carnivores at the top of food webs—exert disproportionate effects on ecosystems through trophic cascades: their removal releases prey from predation pressure, driving cascading changes in vegetation, nutrient cycling, fire regimes, disease dynamics, and carbon sequestration. The decline or extirpation of apex predators, driven primarily by human hunting and habitat loss, represents one of humanity's most pervasive influences on nature. Predator reintroduction can restore top-down regulation and reverse trophic downgrading in degraded ecosystems.
Also known as: carnivory, top-down control, apex predation, predator-prey interaction

Mechanisms

Apex Predator Regulation of Prey Populations
Large apex carnivores regulate prey population size and behavior through direct lethal effects (consumption) and non-lethal effects (risk effects modifying prey habitat use, foraging behavior, and vigilance). Both direct and indirect predation effects cascade through food webs to alter plant community structure, vegetation biomass, and abiotic ecosystem processes. The loss of large carnivores—trophic downgrading—represents humankind's most pervasive influence on natural systems.

Trophic Cascade via Prey Release
When apex predators are extirpated, prey populations increase (prey release) and alter their foraging behavior, generating trophic cascades that propagate downward through food webs to plants, soils, and abiotic processes. Trophic cascades mediated by large carnivores affect vegetation biomass, tree recruitment, riparian plant communities, stream morphology, carbon sequestration, disease prevalence, and wildfire frequency. Their magnitude depends on food web complexity, ecosystem productivity, and whether multiple carnivore species are present.

Non-Lethal Predation Risk Effects on Prey Behavior
Predation risk modifies prey behavior even in the absence of direct predation mortality. Prey shift habitat use, reduce foraging time, and increase vigilance in response to predator presence—the "ecology of fear." These behavioral effects propagate as landscape-scale changes in vegetation through reduced herbivory in high-risk patches, potentially equivalent in magnitude to direct predation effects on prey populations.

Drivers & Stressors

Human hunting and persecution of large carnivores ANTHROPOGENIC
Hunting, predator persecution, and retaliatory killing in response to livestock depredation are the primary drivers of apex predator population decline globally. Large carnivores are extirpated from the majority of their historic range, reducing top-down trophic control across broad geographic areas.

Prey availability and habitat quality ABIOTIC
Prey population size, diversity, and spatial distribution determine the carrying capacity for predator populations and the intensity of predation pressure. Habitat fragmentation reduces prey availability and disrupts predator hunting ranges, weakening top-down control even where predators are nominally present.

Food web complexity and trophic redundancy ABIOTIC
The number of trophic levels, the presence of alternative predators, and the degree of trophic omnivory determine how strongly trophic cascades propagate following apex predator loss. High trophic redundancy (multiple species at each level) dampens cascade strength; simple food webs produce strong, predictable cascades from single predator losses.

Interventions

Apex predator reintroduction and rewilding RESTORATION
Reintroducing locally extirpated apex predators (wolves, cougars, lynx, large felids) restores top-down trophic control, prey population regulation, and the cascading ecosystem effects of predation on vegetation, nutrient cycling, and biodiversity. Rewilding programs in North America and Europe have demonstrated measurable ecosystem recovery following predator restoration.

Large carnivore coexistence policies and human-wildlife conflict mitigation PROTECTION
Livestock protection measures (guardian dogs, predator-proof corrals, compensation schemes) and public education reduce retaliatory killing of large carnivores, enabling predator population recovery in human- dominated landscapes and restoring trophic regulation at landscape scale.
